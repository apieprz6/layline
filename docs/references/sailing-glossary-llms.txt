# Sailing Terminology for AI Assistants

This reference provides sailing terminology for generating domain-accurate code, prompts, UI text, and race briefings in the Layline application.

## Wind Conditions (Layline Classification)

Light air: 0-8 knots
  - Challenging conditions requiring finesse and technique
  - Full sails deployed, minimal heel angle
  - Focus on maintaining boat speed over pointing high
  - Difficult to maintain steerage and momentum
  - Crew weight forward to reduce drag

Medium air: 9-15 knots
  - Optimal racing conditions, most comfortable
  - Full sail inventory, good boat speed achievable
  - Standard rig settings applicable
  - Classic racing tactics work well
  - Most predictable sailing conditions

Heavy air: 16-22 knots
  - Physically demanding, crew working hard
  - Consider reefing mainsail or changing to smaller jib
  - Tighter rig settings (increased backstay tension)
  - Crew weight maximized on windward rail
  - Focus on boat control and survivability

Storm conditions: 23+ knots
  - Dangerous conditions, safety paramount
  - Strongly consider not racing
  - Risk of equipment failure and crew injury
  - If racing, reef heavily and use storm sails

## Rig Setup Terms

Backstay: Adjustable cable/wire running from top of mast to stern
  - Controls mast bend and forestay tension
  - Tighter backstay = flatter mainsail, more forestay tension
  - Looser backstay = fuller mainsail, less pointing ability

Cunningham: Downhaul line controlling luff tension on mainsail
  - Pulls sail down to adjust draft position
  - More tension in heavier air to flatten sail
  - Named after inventor Briggs Cunningham

Outhaul: Line controlling foot tension of mainsail
  - Adjusts depth of mainsail along bottom edge
  - Tight outhaul = flatter sail for heavy air
  - Loose outhaul = fuller sail for light air

Vang (Boom Vang): Tackle or hydraulic system controlling boom angle
  - Prevents boom from lifting
  - Controls mainsail leech twist
  - Critical for downwind sailing

Prebend: Standing curve built into mast
  - Affects mainsail shape and luff curve
  - More prebend = flatter mainsail

Shroud tension: Compression on side stays (shrouds) supporting mast
  - Affects mast stiffness and bend characteristics
  - Adjusted with turnbuckles at chainplates

## Sail Trim Terms

Jib sheet: Line controlling angle of jib (foresail) to centerline
  - Primary control for jib trim
  - Adjusted constantly while racing
  - Led through blocks to cockpit winches

Main sheet: Line controlling angle of main boom to centerline
  - Primary control for mainsail trim
  - Typically led to traveler car or direct to cockpit

Traveler: Track (usually on deck) allowing main boom attachment point to move side-to-side
  - Controls boom position athwartships
  - Key tool for pointing ability and power management
  - Up to windward = more pointing, down to leeward = more power

Halyard: Line that raises and tensions sails
  - Jib halyard, main halyard, spinnaker halyard
  - Proper tension affects luff tension and sail shape

Twist: Amount top of sail is turned off (eased) relative to bottom
  - More twist = less power, more forgiving
  - Less twist = more power, requires precision
  - Controlled by sheet tension and vang

Leech: Trailing (aft) edge of sail
  - Critical for power and drag
  - Leech flutter indicates need for adjustment

Luff: Leading (forward) edge of sail
  - Luff wrinkles indicate over-trimmed sail
  - Should be smooth and unwrinkled when properly trimmed

Telltales: Yarn or ribbon indicators on sails showing airflow
  - Used to judge sail trim and wind angle
  - Both sides should flow aft when properly trimmed

## Tactical Terms

Windward: Upwind direction (toward the source of wind)
  - "Windward mark" is the upwind turning mark
  - "To windward" means upwind of another boat

Leeward: Downwind direction (away from the wind source)
  - "Leeward mark" is the downwind turning mark
  - "To leeward" means downwind of another boat

Port tack: Sailing with wind coming from port (left) side
  - Port tack boat must give way to starboard tack boat
  - Right-of-way rules critical in racing

Starboard tack: Sailing with wind coming from starboard (right) side
  - Has right-of-way over port tack boats
  - Can call "starboard" to claim right-of-way

Lift: Wind shift that allows boat to point higher toward windward mark
  - Favorable shift on current tack
  - Tack immediately on a lift to maximize gain
  - "Getting lifted" is advantageous

Header: Wind shift that forces boat to turn away from windward mark
  - Unfavorable shift on current tack
  - Consider tacking on a header
  - "Getting headed" usually indicates time to tack

Favored side: Side of racecourse expected to have advantage
  - Based on predicted wind shifts, current patterns, or local geography
  - Strategic decision about which way to go after start

Layline: Course angle that will reach the mark without additional tacks
  - "On the layline" means one more tack gets you to mark
  - Approaching layline too early is often disadvantageous

Tack (verb): Turning bow through wind to change from port to starboard tack or vice versa
  - "Tacking duel" refers to covering maneuver
  - "Short tacking" means frequent tacks

Gybe/Jibe: Turning stern through wind while sailing downwind
  - Changes which side boom/spinnaker are on
  - More dangerous maneuver than tacking

Covering: Staying between competitor and next mark
  - Defensive tactic to protect position
  - May involve frequent tacks to maintain cover

## Course Types

Windward-Leeward (W/L): Two-leg course
  - First leg upwind to windward mark
  - Second leg downwind back to leeward mark (often start/finish)
  - Repeat for multiple laps
  - Most common Wednesday night format

Triangle: Three-sided course with reaching legs
  - Windward mark, wing marks, leeward mark
  - Includes reaching (wind from side) in addition to upwind/downwind
  - Tests full range of sailing angles

Olympic: Combination of windward-leeward and triangle
  - Multiple legs including windward, leeward, and reaching
  - More complex than simple W/L

## Points of Sail

Close-hauled: Sailing as close to wind as possible (typically 35-45° off wind)
  - Upwind sailing
  - Requires precise trim and helmsmanship
  - Layline angles are typically close-hauled

Reaching: Sailing with wind from the side (beam reach = 90° to wind)
  - Fast point of sail
  - More forgiving trim than close-hauled
  - Broad reach = wind aft of beam

Running: Sailing directly downwind
  - Wind from directly behind
  - Often slowest point of sail
  - Spinnaker typically deployed if available

## Local Lake Michigan Context

CHII2 (Harrison Dever Crib): Offshore lighthouse/platform used as wind measurement station
  - Located in Lake Michigan off Chicago
  - Wind measured at 85 feet elevation
  - Readings typically 20-30% higher than surface wind
  - Excellent for direction and trend analysis
  - NOT representative of actual on-water wind speed

Purdue Buoy (45198): Research buoy measuring on-water conditions
  - Seasonal deployment (May through October)
  - Most accurate on-water wind speed when operational
  - Surface-level measurements
  - Often offline during winter months

Shoreline thermal effect: Temperature differential between land and water
  - Cold lake water vs warm land creates local wind patterns
  - Affects wind speed and direction near shore
  - Evening races (Wednesday 7pm) often impacted
  - Wind can "clock" (rotate) as temperature changes

Evening wind: Typical Wednesday night race conditions
  - Wind often lighter as sun sets
  - More shifty and unstable than midday
  - Thermal effects from land/water temperature difference
  - Direction changes as temperature gradient shifts

Lake effect: Weather phenomenon unique to Great Lakes
  - Cold lake water modifies air masses crossing it
  - Creates distinct wind patterns
  - Spring and fall most pronounced

Navy Pier Racing Circle: Race area off Navy Pier, Chicago
  - Location: approximately 41.89°N, 87.60°W
  - Urban harbor setting
  - Wind influenced by buildings and shoreline
  - Typical Wednesday night race venue

## PHRF Racing Context

PHRF: Performance Handicap Racing Fleet
  - Handicap system allowing different boat types to race together
  - Each boat has rating (seconds per mile)
  - Corrected time determines winner, not finish order
  - Typical rating range: 80-250+ seconds per mile
  - Lower number = faster boat, higher number = slower boat

Corrected time: Elapsed time adjusted by PHRF rating
  - Formula accounts for boat speed potential
  - Allows fair competition between different designs
  - "Time-on-time" or "time-on-distance" calculation methods

One-design racing: All boats are identical (NOT PHRF)
  - Layline is designed for PHRF racing, not one-design
  - Different strategic considerations

## Race Roles and Positions

Skipper/Helmsman: Person steering the boat
  - Primary decision-maker for strategy and tactics
  - Controls tiller or wheel
  - Calls for tacks and gybes

Crew: Team members handling sails and lines
  - Trimmers (jib trimmer, main trimmer)
  - Pit crew (manages halyards and controls)
  - Bow crew (handles spinnaker, foresails)
  - Rail meat (crew weight on windward rail in heavy air)

Tactician: Crew member responsible for race strategy
  - Advises on course decisions
  - Monitors competition
  - Calls wind shifts and strategic moves
  - Layline's AI briefing assists the tactician

## Common Abbreviations

SOG: Speed Over Ground (actual speed relative to earth)
COG: Course Over Ground (actual direction relative to earth)
VMG: Velocity Made Good (speed component toward mark)
  - Can be VMG upwind or VMG downwind
  - More important than raw boat speed

TWS: True Wind Speed (actual wind speed)
TWD: True Wind Direction (actual wind direction, typically magnetic)
AWS: Apparent Wind Speed (wind speed as felt on moving boat)
AWA: Apparent Wind Angle (angle of apparent wind to boat centerline)

GPS: Global Positioning System (navigation)
VHF: Very High Frequency radio (marine communication)
RC: Race Committee (officials running the race)

## Sail Types

Main/Mainsail: Primary sail attached to mast and boom
Jib: Foresail attached to forestay (smaller than genoa)
Genoa: Large overlapping foresail (bigger than jib)
Spinnaker: Large downwind sail (symmetrical or asymmetrical)
Storm jib: Small heavy-weather foresail
Reefed main: Mainsail with area reduced for heavy weather

## Weather and Wind Terms

Knots: Nautical miles per hour (standard wind speed unit in sailing)
  - 1 knot = 1.15 mph = 1.85 km/h
  - Always use knots, not mph, in sailing context

Gust: Temporary increase in wind speed
  - Typically lasts seconds to minutes
  - Important for crew coordination

Lull: Temporary decrease in wind speed
  - Opposite of gust
  - Requires different sail trim

Frontal passage: Weather front moving through area
  - Often brings significant wind shift
  - Can dramatically change conditions mid-race

Velocity header: Wind shift where wind also increases
  - Boat appears to be headed but wind speed increased
  - May not need to tack despite heading change

## Racing Rules Context (brief)

Port/Starboard: Starboard tack has right-of-way
Windward/Leeward: Leeward boat has right-of-way when overlapped
Mark room: Inside boat entitled to room at marks when overlapped
Starting: No crossing start line before gun

Note: Layline focuses on weather/strategy, not detailed rules

## Usage in Layline Application

When generating race briefings:
- Use proper terminology (e.g., "windward mark" not "upwind buoy")
- Reference CHII2 elevation context (85 ft readings)
- Acknowledge when Purdue Buoy is offline (winter)
- Frame advice for PHRF racing on Lake Michigan
- Consider Wednesday evening conditions in tactical advice
- Use knots for all wind speeds
- Apply wind condition classifications (light/medium/heavy/storm)
